Lesson 4.1

Cell Communication

Cells send and receive signals to coordinate behavior, development, and homeostasis.

Understand the mechanism

Direct contact, local signaling, and long-distance endocrine signaling use ligands and receptors; only cells with compatible receptors respond.

Cells communicate by direct junctions, contact-dependent surface molecules, local signaling and long-range hormones. A ligand may be abundant but have no effect on a cell lacking its receptor. Reception is therefore both a molecular recognition event and an essential determinant of cell specificity.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Predict which cells respond to a signal when receptor expression differs.
Check your understanding: Why does only one of two cell types respond to a hormone?

Answer: Only one may express the required receptor or pathway.

Lesson 4.2

Introduction to Signal Transduction

Signal transduction converts ligand binding into a cellular response through reception, transduction, and response.

Understand the mechanism

Membrane receptors detect many hydrophilic signals; intracellular receptors can bind signals that cross the membrane.

A signaling pathway has reception, transduction and response. Hydrophilic ligands usually engage surface receptors; suitable small nonpolar signals may bind intracellular receptors. A pathway can rapidly alter enzyme activity or more slowly change transcription and protein synthesis.

Apply it on the AP exam: Classify stages of a signaling pathway and explain why a signal outside the cell can alter gene expression.
Check your understanding: What is the first step in a typical pathway?

Answer: Reception through ligand–receptor interaction.

Lesson 4.3

Signal Transduction Pathways

Multistep pathways relay and amplify information through protein interactions and second messengers.

Understand the mechanism

Phosphorylation cascades and second messengers such as cAMP and Ca²⁺ can amplify a signal; phosphatases and feedback mechanisms can terminate or tune responses.

Kinases transfer phosphate groups onto proteins and phosphatases remove them; activation can proceed through cascades. Second messengers such as Ca²+ or cAMP distribute a signal inside cells. The same signal can cause different outcomes depending on receptor type, downstream components and gene expression.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict the effect of disrupting a receptor, kinase, second messenger, or phosphatase.
Check your understanding: How could a kinase inhibitor alter a pathway?

Answer: It may block phosphorylation and downstream activation.

Lesson 4.4

Feedback

Negative feedback counteracts deviation from a set point; positive feedback amplifies a process until a stopping event.

Understand the mechanism

Blood-glucose control illustrates negative feedback; childbirth and some blood-clotting steps illustrate positive feedback.

Negative feedback reduces a perturbation, as in many homeostatic mechanisms. Positive feedback reinforces a process, as in some developmental or reproductive cascades, but requires conditions that eventually end the loop. Identify the sensor, control pathway, effector and resulting change when analyzing a feedback diagram.

Apply it on the AP exam: Identify the stimulus, sensor, response, and direction of feedback in a system diagram.
Check your understanding: What distinguishes negative from positive feedback?

Answer: Negative feedback counteracts change; positive feedback amplifies it.

Lesson 4.5

Cell Cycle

The cell cycle includes interphase (G₁, S, G₂) and division; mitosis separates duplicated chromosomes.

Understand the mechanism

DNA replicates during S phase; mitosis proceeds through prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, followed by cytokinesis.

The eukaryotic cell cycle includes G1 growth, S-phase DNA replication, G2 preparation and M-phase mitosis/cytokinesis. Mitosis separates replicated chromosomes into genetically similar daughter nuclei. Stages are prophase, prometaphase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase, followed or accompanied by cytokinesis.

Apply it on the AP exam: Use chromosome/chromatid diagrams to explain how genetically similar daughter cells form.
Check your understanding: In which phase does DNA replication occur?

Answer: S phase.

Lesson 4.6

Regulation of Cell Cycle

Checkpoints coordinate cell division with DNA integrity, growth signals, and spindle attachment.

Understand the mechanism

Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases regulate transitions; mutations in regulatory genes can lead to uncontrolled division.

Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases regulate checkpoints; DNA damage and improper spindle attachment can delay division. Proto-oncogenes normally help promote growth signals, whereas tumor suppressor genes restrain progression or support repair. Mutations that bypass control may contribute to cancer.

Apply it on the AP exam: Interpret cell-cycle data and explain how failure of checkpoints can contribute to cancer.
Check your understanding: Why might a checkpoint mutation contribute to tumors?

Answer: Damaged cells may continue dividing rather than arresting.
