Lesson 2.1

Cell Structure and Function

All cells possess a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, genetic material, and ribosomes; eukaryotes have membrane-bound organelles.

Understand the mechanism

The nucleus houses DNA, ribosomes synthesize protein, rough ER processes secreted/membrane proteins, Golgi modifies and sorts, mitochondria support ATP synthesis, chloroplasts capture light, and lysosomes digest materials.

Prokaryotes generally lack a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles, whereas eukaryotes separate many reactions in compartments. Free ribosomes synthesize many cytosolic proteins; bound ribosomes often make exported, lysosomal or membrane proteins. The endomembrane route proceeds through ER, transport vesicles, Golgi and destinations.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Infer cellular specialization from relative organelle abundance; distinguish prokaryotic cells from eukaryotic cells.
Check your understanding: Which organelles expand in a protein-secreting cell?

Answer: Rough ER and Golgi are typically especially prominent.

Lesson 2.2

Cell Size

Surface area grows with length squared, whereas volume grows with length cubed.

Understand the mechanism

A smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio makes material exchange less efficient; microvilli and flattened or elongated cells increase exchange surface.

For a cube of side s, surface area is 6s² and volume is s³, so SA:V = 6/s. Doubling linear dimensions halves the ratio. Exchange surfaces use folding, branching, thinness or small cell size to reduce transport limitations; volume also influences demand for nutrients and waste removal.

Apply it on the AP exam: Calculate surface-area-to-volume ratios for cubes and explain constraints on cell size.
Check your understanding: What is the SA:V ratio of a cube with side 3 units?

Answer: Area = 54 and volume = 27, so ratio = 2:1.

Lesson 2.3

Plasma Membrane

The fluid mosaic membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins and other components.

Understand the mechanism

Phospholipid heads face water; tails form a hydrophobic interior. Membrane proteins transport substances, receive signals, and help recognize cells; cholesterol influences fluidity in animal membranes.

The fluid mosaic model describes laterally mobile phospholipids and many membrane proteins. Hydrophilic heads face extracellular fluid and cytosol; hydrophobic tails point inward. Integral proteins can act as transporters or receptors, while carbohydrate chains can contribute to recognition.

Apply it on the AP exam: Relate molecular polarity and size to movement across a lipid bilayer.
Check your understanding: Why can CO₂ cross a membrane without a transport protein?

Answer: It is small and nonpolar.

Lesson 2.4

Membrane Permeability

Selective permeability regulates the internal environment of a cell.

Understand the mechanism

Small nonpolar molecules cross easily; ions and most large polar molecules require transport proteins; membrane composition affects passage rates.

Permeability depends on size, electrical charge and polarity. O₂ and CO₂ cross the lipid interior comparatively readily. Water may diffuse slowly through lipids but aquaporins permit rapid movement. Charged ions generally need dedicated proteins even when their concentration difference favors entry.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict permeability changes when a protein channel or phospholipid composition is altered.
Check your understanding: Why can chloride require a channel despite a steep gradient?

Answer: Its charge makes the membrane core unfavorable.

Lesson 2.5

Membrane Transport

Passive transport moves substances down their concentration or electrochemical gradients; active transport requires an energy source.

Understand the mechanism

Simple diffusion and osmosis are passive; pumps use ATP directly or exploit preexisting ion gradients to move solutes against gradients.

Diffusion moves substances down a concentration gradient; net movement decreases as equilibrium approaches, though molecules continue moving randomly. Primary active transport couples ATP hydrolysis to movement. Secondary active transport uses the energy stored in a previously established electrochemical gradient.

Apply it on the AP exam: Identify whether ATP is required and determine direction of net solute or water movement.
Check your understanding: Can active transport move a solute against a gradient?

Answer: Yes, using ATP directly or coupled gradient energy.

Lesson 2.6

Facilitated Diffusion

Polar molecules and ions move down their gradients through specific channel or carrier proteins.

Understand the mechanism

Facilitated diffusion does not directly require ATP; channels provide pores while carriers bind and change conformation; transport can saturate.

Channels form water-filled paths for ions or water and may be gated. Carriers bind a solute and undergo conformational change. Because transporter quantity and turnover are finite, facilitated diffusion can show saturation, unlike an idealized simple diffusion model.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain why adding more carriers increases the rate until another factor becomes limiting.
Check your understanding: What happens if all carriers are occupied?

Answer: The transport rate approaches its capacity and saturates.

Lesson 2.7

Tonicity and Osmoregulation

Water moves across selectively permeable membranes in response to differences in effective solute concentration.

Understand the mechanism

Hypotonic surroundings cause animal cells to swell; hypertonic surroundings cause shrinkage; plant cells develop turgor pressure in hypotonic conditions.

Tonicity predicts cell-volume change based on nonpenetrating solutes, not total solute particles alone. In a hypertonic solution, water leaves a typical animal cell; in hypotonic solution, water enters. A plant cell becomes turgid as its wall counters osmotic entry; plasmolysis can occur in hypertonic conditions.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict mass or volume change in dialysis tubing, red blood cells, and plant cells; distinguish osmolarity from tonicity.
Check your understanding: What happens to a red blood cell in a hypertonic medium?

Answer: Water leaves and the cell shrinks.

Lesson 2.8

Mechanisms of Transport

Cells move bulk material using vesicles in endocytosis and exocytosis.

Understand the mechanism

Endocytosis internalizes material; exocytosis fuses a vesicle with the plasma membrane to release contents; both require cellular energy and membrane remodeling.

Phagocytosis engulfs larger particles; pinocytosis takes up extracellular fluid; receptor-mediated endocytosis selectively internalizes cargo. Exocytosis releases cargo through membrane fusion and also contributes membrane components to the cell surface. Vesicular transport moves material that cannot cross the bilayer directly.

Apply it on the AP exam: Identify which mechanism a cell uses to ingest particles or secrete proteins.
Check your understanding: Which pathway exports a secreted enzyme?

Answer: Exocytosis by vesicle fusion at the membrane.

Lesson 2.9

Cell Compartmentalization

Internal membranes isolate reactions and establish distinct chemical conditions.

Understand the mechanism

Compartmentalization increases efficiency, separates incompatible processes, and enables electrochemical gradients across membranes.

Compartmentalization can maintain different pH values, ion concentrations, enzymes and substrates. The inner mitochondrial membrane supports proton-gradient formation; lysosomes maintain acidic interiors; chloroplast thylakoids separate luminal and stromal conditions. A leak can dissipate a gradient even if enzymes remain intact.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain why disrupting an organelle membrane can reduce a pathway's efficiency.
Check your understanding: What does a mitochondrial inner-membrane proton leak do?

Answer: It weakens the gradient and generally reduces ATP synthase output.

Lesson 2.10

Origins of Cell Compartmentalization

The endosymbiotic theory proposes that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from free-living prokaryotes.

Understand the mechanism

Evidence includes double membranes, circular DNA, bacterial-like ribosomes, and division resembling binary fission; internal membrane infolding may explain other components.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA and ribosomes, divide by fission-like processes, and have bacterial relationships inferred from molecular evidence. The original engulfment produced a mutually beneficial partnership followed by extensive gene transfer to the nucleus.

Apply it on the AP exam: Evaluate evidence supporting endosymbiosis rather than relying on superficial similarities.
Check your understanding: Give two lines of endosymbiotic evidence.

Answer: Circular DNA and bacterial-like ribosomes, among others.
