Lesson 3.1

Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts that lower activation energy without changing the overall free-energy change of a reaction.

Understand the mechanism

Substrates bind specific active sites; induced fit, substrate orientation, and transition-state stabilization speed reactions; enzymes are not consumed.

Activation energy is the energetic barrier between reactants and products. Enzymes stabilize transition states and orient substrates but do not change ΔG or shift the final equilibrium. Binding specificity emerges from the active site’s shape and chemistry, rather than a perfectly rigid lock-and-key fit.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Interpret reaction-energy diagrams and distinguish activation energy from ΔG.
Check your understanding: Does an enzyme change reaction ΔG?

Answer: No; it lowers activation energy for forward and reverse reactions.

Lesson 3.2

Environmental Impacts on Enzyme Function

Enzyme activity depends on temperature, pH, substrate availability, and inhibitors.

Understand the mechanism

Extreme heat or pH can alter active-site structure; competitive inhibitors bind active sites and noncompetitive inhibitors can act elsewhere; saturation limits maximum reaction rate.

At low substrate concentrations rate usually rises with concentration; once active sites are occupied, rate approaches a maximum. Temperature can initially increase collisions but high heat damages protein structure. Inhibitors and pH perturbations change kinetics. A valid experiment holds enzyme amount, time and other conditions constant.

Apply it on the AP exam: Interpret enzyme-rate graphs and identify controls and independent/dependent variables.
Check your understanding: Why can reaction speed fall above an optimum temperature?

Answer: The enzyme can lose its functional conformation.

Lesson 3.3

Cellular Energy

Cells couple exergonic reactions to endergonic work using ATP and molecular gradients.

Understand the mechanism

ATP hydrolysis can power reactions, transport, and movement; redox reactions transfer electrons through carriers; metabolic pathways regulate energy use.

Exergonic reactions have negative ΔG under the specified conditions; endergonic reactions require an energy input. Cells couple unfavorable steps to favorable ones, often via ATP hydrolysis. Electron carriers NADH and FADH₂ store transferable reducing power; their reoxidation helps sustain metabolism.

Apply it on the AP exam: Distinguish energy transfer from energy creation and predict effects of ATP or electron-carrier shortages.
Check your understanding: What drives phosphorylation of a target protein by ATP?

Answer: Coupling ATP hydrolysis to the otherwise unfavorable process.

Lesson 3.4

Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis captures light energy to make sugars from carbon dioxide; oxygen is released from water.

Understand the mechanism

Light reactions in thylakoid membranes produce ATP and NADPH and release O₂; the Calvin cycle in the stroma fixes CO₂ using ATP and NADPH.

Photosystem II extracts electrons from water and releases O₂; light-driven electron transfer generates a proton gradient, while photosystem I helps reduce NADP+ to NADPH. ATP synthase makes ATP in thylakoid membranes. In the Calvin cycle, rubisco fixes CO₂ to RuBP; ATP and NADPH support reduction and RuBP regeneration.

Apply it on the AP exam: Trace matter and energy separately, identify inputs and outputs, and predict effects of blocked electron transport.
Check your understanding: Which molecule supplies released O₂ in photosynthesis?

Answer: Water.

Lesson 3.5

Cellular Respiration

Aerobic respiration oxidizes fuel and uses the resulting electron transfers to drive ATP synthesis.

Understand the mechanism

Glycolysis occurs in cytosol; pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle produce reduced carriers; oxidative phosphorylation uses an electron transport chain, proton gradient, and ATP synthase; O₂ is terminal electron acceptor.

Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate in cytosol with net ATP and NADH production. After pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle releases CO₂ and loads electron carriers. The electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and ATP synthase harnesses their return. Fermentation regenerates NAD+ without using oxygen as a terminal acceptor.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain chemiosmosis, distinguish substrate-level from oxidative phosphorylation, and predict effects of an oxygen shortage.
Check your understanding: Why does oxygen deprivation impair oxidative phosphorylation?

Answer: Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in aerobic electron transport.
