Structure determines function

Follow polarity → intermolecular forces → macromolecular structure → function. The exam often asks you to explain why a chemical difference produces a biological consequence.

Practice scientific skills: Water-property investigations; comparing membrane effects of lipid saturation; interpreting protein denaturation.

Scientific reasoning matters as much as vocabulary: interpret a model, identify variables and controls, calculate when appropriate, and use evidence to support a biological explanation.

Lesson 1.1

Structure of Water and Hydrogen Bonding

Water is polar: oxygen is partially negative and hydrogens are partially positive; hydrogen bonds form among water molecules.

Understand the mechanism

Hydrogen bonding explains cohesion, adhesion, surface tension, high specific heat, heat of vaporization, and ice being less dense than liquid water.

Water molecules orient their partial charges toward opposite charges on neighboring molecules. Cohesion helps sustain a continuous water column in xylem; adhesion helps water cling to cell walls. Because breaking hydrogen bonds absorbs thermal energy, water resists rapid temperature swings. Ice forms an open crystalline structure and floats, insulating lakes beneath it.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Predict how breaking hydrogen bonds or changing environmental temperature affects aquatic life and biological processes.
Check your understanding: Why does a lake freeze from the top downward?

Answer: Ice is less dense because hydrogen bonds maintain an open lattice, leaving liquid water underneath.

Lesson 1.2

Elements of Life

Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur are essential elements; carbon forms four stable covalent bonds.

Understand the mechanism

Carbon skeletons and functional groups create molecular diversity; nitrogen appears in amino groups, phosphorus in phosphate groups, and sulfur in some amino acids.

Carbon can form single and double bonds, chains, rings, and branching structures. Functional groups alter reactivity: hydroxyl increases polarity, carboxyl can donate H+, amino can accept H+, and phosphate contributes negative charge. Nutrient limitation is a matter of available atoms, not merely available calories.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain how availability of nitrogen or phosphorus can constrain biological growth.
Check your understanding: Why is carbon unusually versatile in biomolecules?

Answer: Four covalent bonds allow varied stable skeletons and functional groups.

Lesson 1.3

Introduction to Macromolecules

Macromolecules include carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids; biological structure determines function.

Understand the mechanism

Dehydration synthesis removes water to link subunits; hydrolysis adds water to break covalent linkages. Lipids are not true polymers.

Condensation (dehydration) reactions create covalent linkages between monomers while releasing water; hydrolysis uses water to split them. Identify building blocks by chemistry rather than assuming every large biomolecule is a polymer. A polymer’s sequence and three-dimensional shape influence how it behaves in cells.

Apply it on the AP exam: Distinguish monomers from polymers and recognize why changing a molecule's structure changes its function.
Check your understanding: How would hydrolysis affect a polysaccharide?

Answer: Water helps cleave glycosidic bonds, producing smaller sugars.

Lesson 1.4

Carbohydrates

Monosaccharides are sugar monomers; disaccharides and polysaccharides have glycosidic bonds.

Understand the mechanism

Starch and glycogen store energy; cellulose provides plant cell-wall structure; differences in glycosidic linkages change digestibility and shape.

Alpha and beta glycosidic linkages determine the shape and biological role of glucose polymers. Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) function in storage; cellulose uses beta-1,4 linkages to make strong fibrils. Most humans lack enzymes to hydrolyze these cellulose bonds.

Apply it on the AP exam: Compare energy-storage polysaccharides with structural cellulose and explain why enzymes are substrate-specific.
Check your understanding: Why can humans digest starch but not cellulose?

Answer: Their glycosidic linkage geometry requires different enzymes; humans lack cellulase.

Lesson 1.5

Lipids

Lipids are largely nonpolar and include triglycerides, phospholipids, and steroids.

Understand the mechanism

Fatty-acid saturation alters packing and fluidity; amphipathic phospholipids self-assemble into bilayers with hydrophilic heads outward and hydrophobic tails inward.

A triglyceride contains glycerol and three fatty acids joined by ester bonds. Saturated chains are straighter and pack tightly; cis-unsaturated chains bend and reduce packing. Phospholipids form bilayers spontaneously in water. Steroid molecules contain fused carbon rings; cholesterol buffers animal membrane fluidity.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict membrane fluidity changes when unsaturated fatty acids increase; relate structure to permeability.
Check your understanding: Why do unsaturated tails usually increase membrane fluidity?

Answer: Double-bond kinks reduce tight phospholipid packing.

Lesson 1.6

Nucleic Acids

DNA and RNA consist of nucleotide units: a pentose sugar, phosphate group, and nitrogenous base.

Understand the mechanism

Phosphodiester bonds form sugar-phosphate backbones; DNA is typically double-stranded and antiparallel; complementary bases pair A–T and C–G in DNA (A–U in RNA).

A nucleotide contains sugar, phosphate and a nitrogenous base. The 5′-to-3′ polarity of each strand arises from the sugar-phosphate backbone. Antiparallel DNA strands have complementary bases paired by hydrogen bonds; the covalent phosphodiester backbone is much stronger than interstrand hydrogen bonding.

Apply it on the AP exam: Use sequence complementarity to explain information storage and how changing a sequence can affect gene products.
Check your understanding: What sequence complements 5′-ATGC-3′ in antiparallel DNA?

Answer: 3′-TACG-5′.

Lesson 1.7

Proteins

Amino acids share amino and carboxyl groups but have distinct R groups; peptide bonds link them.

Understand the mechanism

Primary sequence drives folding into secondary, tertiary, and sometimes quaternary structures through hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic effects, and disulfide bridges.

Each amino acid has a characteristic side chain. Peptide bonds form the polypeptide backbone, but folding depends on noncovalent interactions and sometimes disulfide bonds. Primary structure constrains higher-level structure; a substitution can be neutral or disruptive depending on position, charge and environment.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict consequences of a substitution, pH shift, or temperature change on protein folding and biological function.
Check your understanding: Why might a single amino-acid substitution matter?

Answer: A changed side chain can alter intermolecular interactions and folding.
