Lesson 6.1

DNA and RNA Structure

DNA and RNA store and convey genetic information using base sequences.

Understand the mechanism

DNA typically has deoxyribose and thymine; RNA has ribose and uracil; strands have 5′ to 3′ directionality and complementary base pairing.

DNA uses deoxyribose and thymine; RNA uses ribose and uracil. Purines are A and G; pyrimidines are C, T and U. Antiparallel complementarity helps explain template-directed copying. Base sequence encodes information but sequence alone does not determine expression levels.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Interpret polarity, template direction, and base-pair relationships in a diagram.
Check your understanding: What base is in RNA where DNA has thymine?

Answer: Uracil.

Lesson 6.2

DNA Replication

DNA replication is semiconservative: each daughter double helix contains one parental strand.

Understand the mechanism

Helicase unwinds DNA; primase makes primers; polymerase extends 5′→3′; leading-strand synthesis is continuous and lagging-strand synthesis forms Okazaki fragments joined by ligase.

DNA replication is semiconservative. Helicase unwinds DNA, primase makes RNA primers, DNA polymerase extends new DNA only 5′→3′, and ligase seals fragments. The leading strand is synthesized more continuously than the lagging strand, which uses Okazaki fragments. Proofreading and repair limit errors.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict impacts of inhibiting helicase, polymerase, or ligase, and explain complementary copying.
Check your understanding: Why is the lagging strand discontinuous?

Answer: DNA polymerase extends only 5′→3′ on antiparallel templates.

Lesson 6.3

Transcription and RNA Processing

RNA polymerase produces RNA using a DNA template; eukaryotic primary transcripts undergo processing.

Understand the mechanism

RNA is synthesized 5′→3′; a 5′ cap, poly-A tail, and splicing help form mature eukaryotic mRNA; alternative splicing can produce different products.

RNA polymerase builds RNA using a DNA template. The template strand is read 3′→5′ while RNA grows 5′→3′. Eukaryotic pre-mRNA processing typically adds a 5′ cap, 3′ poly-A tail and removes introns through splicing; alternative splicing can yield distinct products.

Apply it on the AP exam: Transcribe DNA accurately and explain why one gene can yield different proteins.
Check your understanding: What does alternative splicing change?

Answer: Which exons are included in a mature RNA transcript.

Lesson 6.4

Translation

Ribosomes read mRNA codons to assemble polypeptides through tRNA anticodon pairing.

Understand the mechanism

Translation starts at a start codon, elongates through peptide bonds, and stops at a stop codon; the genetic code is redundant but not ambiguous.

Ribosomes read mRNA codons in the 5′→3′ direction. tRNA anticodons base-pair with codons and bring amino acids. Translation normally begins at AUG and ends at a stop codon; peptide bonds form as a polypeptide extends from its amino terminus to carboxyl terminus.

Apply it on the AP exam: Translate an mRNA sequence using a codon table and analyze frameshift versus substitution mutations.
Check your understanding: What does a stop codon specify?

Answer: Translation termination rather than an amino acid.

Lesson 6.5

Regulation of Gene Expression

Regulation controls when, where, and how much gene product is made.

Understand the mechanism

Transcription factors, regulatory DNA, chromatin accessibility, RNA processing, and mRNA stability influence expression; bacterial operons coordinate related genes.

Bacterial operons coordinate transcription of multiple related genes: repressors and activators influence polymerase access. Eukaryotic regulation involves chromatin accessibility, transcription factors, enhancers, RNA processing, RNA stability and translation. Methylation and histone modification can change accessibility without altering the DNA sequence.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict changes in transcription or protein abundance when regulators are modified.
Check your understanding: Can a gene be silenced without changing DNA bases?

Answer: Yes, via regulatory factors and chromatin state.

Lesson 6.6

Gene Expression and Cell Specialization

Differentiated cells generally have the same genome but different expression profiles.

Understand the mechanism

Cell signals and regulatory networks activate or repress sets of genes, establishing specialized cellular structures and functions.

Differentiated cells usually share essentially the same genome but express different gene sets. Inductive signals activate regulatory networks that establish specialized cell identity. Disrupting a key transcription factor can change developmental pathways even though the underlying DNA remains present.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain tissue-specific protein expression without assuming different cells necessarily carry different DNA.
Check your understanding: Why do muscle and nerve cells differ?

Answer: Different subsets of genes are expressed.

Lesson 6.7

Mutations

Mutations alter nucleotide sequences and can have neutral, harmful, or beneficial effects depending on context.

Understand the mechanism

Substitutions may be silent, missense, or nonsense; insertions/deletions may shift reading frames; only heritable mutations in germ cells are passed to offspring in sexually reproducing organisms.

Point substitutions may be synonymous, missense or nonsense; insertions and deletions may cause frameshifts unless in multiples of three. Mutations in regulatory sequences can affect gene expression without changing protein coding sequence. Germline mutations may be inherited; somatic mutations usually are not.

Apply it on the AP exam: Determine how a specific change alters codons, protein sequence, and potentially phenotype.
Check your understanding: Can a synonymous mutation alter phenotype?

Answer: Often no amino-acid change, but effects on splicing or expression are possible.

Lesson 6.8

Biotechnology

Biotechnology uses molecular tools to analyze and modify nucleic acids.

Understand the mechanism

PCR amplifies DNA using primers and thermal cycling; gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size; restriction enzymes and DNA sequencing reveal genetic patterns; CRISPR systems can target DNA.

Gel electrophoresis separates nucleic-acid fragments largely by size; shorter fragments generally travel farther through the gel. PCR amplifies selected DNA using primers, thermostable polymerase and temperature cycling. DNA sequencing, restriction enzymes, plasmids and CRISPR-based methods have distinct uses and limitations.

Apply it on the AP exam: Interpret banding patterns, choose appropriate controls, and recognize limits of conclusions from molecular evidence.
Check your understanding: Why do shorter fragments usually move farther on gels?

Answer: They pass through gel pores more easily.
