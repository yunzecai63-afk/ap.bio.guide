Lesson 5.1

Meiosis

Meiosis produces haploid cells from diploid precursors by one DNA replication and two divisions.

Understand the mechanism

Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids; chromosome number is reduced in the first division.

Meiosis consists of one DNA replication followed by two divisions. Homologous chromosomes pair in prophase I; homologs separate during meiosis I, and sister chromatids usually separate during meiosis II. Haploid cells result, allowing fertilization to restore the diploid chromosome number.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Track chromosome number and distinguish meiosis from mitosis.
Check your understanding: What separates at anaphase I?

Answer: Homologous chromosomes.

Lesson 5.2

Meiosis and Genetic Diversity

Crossing over, independent assortment, and random fertilization generate genetic variation.

Understand the mechanism

Crossing over occurs between nonsister chromatids of homologs during prophase I; homologous pairs orient randomly at metaphase I.

Crossing over between nonsister chromatids in prophase I creates recombinant chromosomes. Independent assortment reflects random homolog orientation at metaphase I. Random fertilization produces additional combinations. Recombination reshuffles existing alleles, while mutations introduce new allele variants.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict consequences of nondisjunction and explain sources of distinct gametes.
Check your understanding: Where do recombinant chromatids originate?

Answer: Crossing over between nonsister homolog chromatids in prophase I.

Lesson 5.3

Mendelian Genetics

Alleles segregate during gamete formation; alleles of different genes assort independently when genes are unlinked.

Understand the mechanism

Dominant and recessive inheritance can be modeled with Punnett squares and probability rules; a testcross reveals an unknown dominant phenotype genotype.

Alleles segregate into gametes. The multiplication rule helps predict independent events, while the addition rule applies to mutually exclusive alternatives. Test crosses reveal genotypes behind dominant phenotypes. A dihybrid 9:3:3:1 ratio assumes complete dominance, independent assortment and typical viability.

Apply it on the AP exam: Compute genotype and phenotype probabilities, while checking whether independence is justified.
Check your understanding: For Aa × Aa, what proportion is aa?

Answer: One quarter, assuming Mendelian segregation.

Lesson 5.4

Non-Mendelian Genetics

Many traits depart from simple dominant/recessive single-gene inheritance.

Understand the mechanism

Incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles, sex linkage, linked genes, and polygenic inheritance produce varied patterns.

Incomplete dominance produces an intermediate heterozygous phenotype; codominance shows contributions from both alleles. Multiple alleles exist within populations, although individuals usually carry two alleles at an autosomal locus. Linkage reduces independent assortment; sex-linked inheritance and epistasis alter expected ratios.

Apply it on the AP exam: Analyze pedigrees and distinguish sex-linked and autosomal patterns; recognize that linked genes are not independent.
Check your understanding: How does codominance differ from incomplete dominance?

Answer: Both contributions appear versus an intermediate phenotype.

Lesson 5.5

Environmental Effects on Phenotype

Phenotype results from genotype and environment, including interactions between them.

Understand the mechanism

Temperature, nutrition, sunlight, and other conditions can influence expression of heritable traits without necessarily changing DNA sequence.

Phenotype reflects genotype together with environment and developmental context. Temperature, nutrition and other factors can modify gene expression or physiological outcomes. Continuous traits often result from multiple genes and environmental contributions; do not infer purely genetic inheritance from family resemblance alone.

Apply it on the AP exam: Use reaction norms or data comparing environments to separate genotype effects from environmental effects.
Check your understanding: Can identical genotypes produce different phenotypes?

Answer: Yes, environmental differences can affect expression and development.
