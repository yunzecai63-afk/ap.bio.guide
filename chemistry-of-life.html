Water: the molecule that makes life possible

Why water is polar

  • Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen.
  • Electrons are shared unequally in water.
  • Oxygen becomes slightly negative, and hydrogens become slightly positive.
  • This polarity allows hydrogen bonding between water molecules.
Water’s polarity is the reason it has unusual life-supporting properties.

Water polarity

Key properties of water

  • Cohesion: water sticks to water
  • Adhesion: water sticks to other polar surfaces
  • High specific heat: resists temperature change
  • Heat of vaporization: evaporation cools surfaces
  • Excellent solvent: surrounds ions and polar molecules
  • Ice is less dense than liquid water: important for aquatic life

Macromolecules

Biological macromolecules are large carbon-based molecules that support life. They differ in structure, building blocks, and function.

Carbohydrates

  • Made mostly of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in about a 1:2:1 ratio
  • Monomers: monosaccharides
  • Examples of monomers: glucose, fructose, galactose
  • Disaccharides form when 2 monosaccharides join together
  • Polysaccharides are long chains used for storage or structure
  • Starch: energy storage in plants
  • Glycogen: energy storage in animals
  • Cellulose: structural support in plant cell walls
  • Main role: short-term energy and structural support
Carbohydrates are great for quick energy and can also help build strong structures like plant cell walls.

Lipids

  • Lipids are mostly nonpolar and hydrophobic
  • They are not true polymers because they are not built from repeating monomers in the same way as proteins or nucleic acids
  • Triglycerides: glycerol + 3 fatty acids
  • Phospholipids: major part of cell membranes
  • Steroids: include cholesterol and some hormones
  • Saturated fats: no double bonds, usually more solid
  • Unsaturated fats: one or more double bonds, usually more liquid
  • Main role: long-term energy storage, membranes, insulation, signaling
Phospholipids are especially important because they form the phospholipid bilayer of cell membranes.

Proteins

  • Monomers: amino acids
  • Amino acids are joined by peptide bonds
  • Contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and sometimes sulfur
  • Functions include enzymes, transport, structure, movement, defense, and signaling
  • Primary structure: amino acid sequence
  • Secondary structure: alpha helices and beta pleated sheets
  • Tertiary structure: 3D folding of one polypeptide
  • Quaternary structure: multiple polypeptide chains together
  • Protein shape determines protein function
If a protein changes shape, it may not work correctly anymore. This is why structure is so important.

Nucleic Acids

  • Monomers: nucleotides
  • Each nucleotide has a sugar, phosphate group, and nitrogenous base
  • DNA: stores hereditary information
  • RNA: helps use genetic information to build proteins
  • DNA bases: adenine, thymine, cytosine, guanine
  • RNA bases: adenine, uracil, cytosine, guanine
  • DNA is usually double-stranded; RNA is usually single-stranded
  • Main role: storing and transmitting genetic information
Nucleic acids connect chemistry to heredity because they store the instructions for life.

Building and breaking molecules

Dehydration synthesis

Builds larger molecules by removing water.

Monomer + monomer → polymer + H₂O

Hydrolysis

Breaks larger molecules apart by adding water.

Polymer + H₂O → smaller molecules

Quick review

Must-know ideas for Unit 1

  • Water is polar, so it forms hydrogen bonds.
  • Hydrogen bonding explains cohesion, adhesion, temperature stability, and solvent properties.
  • Carbohydrates store energy and provide structure.
  • Lipids store long-term energy and form membranes.
  • Proteins are made of amino acids and depend on shape.
  • Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information.
  • Dehydration builds polymers; hydrolysis breaks them apart.
