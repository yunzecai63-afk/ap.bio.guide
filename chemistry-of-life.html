Water: the molecule that makes life possible

Why water is polar

  • Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen.
  • Electrons are shared unequally in water.
  • Oxygen becomes slightly negative, and hydrogens become slightly positive.
  • This polarity allows hydrogen bonding between water molecules.
Water’s polarity is the reason it has unusual life-supporting properties.

Water polarity

Key properties of water

  • Cohesion: water sticks to water
  • Adhesion: water sticks to other polar surfaces
  • High specific heat: resists temperature change
  • Heat of vaporization: evaporation cools surfaces
  • Excellent solvent: surrounds ions and polar molecules
  • Ice is less dense than liquid water: important for aquatic life

Macromolecules

AP Bio students should know each macromolecule’s general structure, building blocks, job, and the labeled parts that show up again and again on tests.

Carbohydrates

General structure: glucose ring
Key thing to notice: lots of –OH groups.
  • Monomer: monosaccharide
  • Examples: glucose, fructose
  • Polymer: polysaccharide
  • Storage forms: starch in plants, glycogen in animals
  • Structural form: cellulose in plant cell walls
  • Main jobs: quick energy, short-term energy storage, structural support
Must remember: hydroxyl groups (–OH), ring shape, monosaccharide vs. polysaccharide.

Lipids

General structure: fatty acids
Key thing to notice: long hydrocarbon chains and a carboxyl group at one end.
  • Not a true polymer: lipids are not made of repeating monomers the same way proteins or nucleic acids are
  • Examples: fats, phospholipids, steroids
  • Triglyceride: glycerol + 3 fatty acids
  • Saturated fatty acid: no C=C double bonds, straighter chain
  • Unsaturated fatty acid: one or more C=C double bonds, bent chain
  • Main jobs: long-term energy storage, insulation, membranes, signaling
  • Phospholipid feature: phosphate-containing head + fatty acid tails
Must remember: hydrocarbon tail, carboxyl group, saturated vs. unsaturated, phosphate group in phospholipids.

Proteins

General structure: amino acid
Key thing to notice: central carbon attached to amino group, carboxyl group, H, and R group.
  • Monomer: amino acid
  • Bond: peptide bond joins amino acids together
  • Contain: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and sometimes sulfur
  • Main jobs: enzymes, transport, structure, movement, signaling, defense
  • Primary structure: amino acid sequence
  • Secondary structure: alpha helices and beta pleated sheets
  • Tertiary structure: overall 3D shape of one polypeptide
  • Quaternary structure: multiple polypeptide chains together
Must remember: amino group (–NH₂), carboxyl group (–COOH), R group, peptide bond, shape determines function.

Nucleic Acids

General structure: nucleotide
Key thing to notice: each nucleotide has a phosphate group, 5-carbon sugar, and nitrogenous base.
  • Monomer: nucleotide
  • DNA job: stores hereditary information
  • RNA job: helps express genetic information to build proteins
  • DNA bases: A, T, C, G
  • RNA bases: A, U, C, G
  • DNA sugar: deoxyribose
  • RNA sugar: ribose
  • Main job: information storage and transfer
Must remember: phosphate group, sugar, nitrogenous base, DNA vs. RNA bases.

Building and breaking molecules

Dehydration synthesis

Builds larger molecules by removing water.

Monomer + monomer → polymer + H₂O

Hydrolysis

Breaks larger molecules apart by adding water.

Polymer + H₂O → smaller molecules

Quick review

Must-know ideas for Unit 1

  • Water is polar, so it forms hydrogen bonds.
  • Hydrogen bonding explains cohesion, adhesion, temperature stability, and solvent properties.
  • Carbohydrates store energy and provide structure.
  • Lipids store long-term energy and form membranes.
  • Proteins are made of amino acids and depend on shape.
  • Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information.
  • Dehydration builds polymers; hydrolysis breaks them apart.
