Lesson 8.1

Responses to the Environment

Organisms respond to stimuli through behavioral, physiological, and developmental changes.

Understand the mechanism

Responses can improve survival and reproduction; timing, signaling, and feedback influence activity, migration, and resource use.

Stimuli can trigger taxis (oriented movement), kinesis (change in movement rate), migration or changes in physiology. Circadian rhythms and seasonal responses integrate external cues with internal timing. Responses need not be consciously chosen to influence survival.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Predict effects of altered cues or environmental conditions on organism behavior.
Check your understanding: Give an example of a response to environmental light.

Answer: Phototaxis or a seasonal behavioral change.

Lesson 8.2

Energy Flow Through Ecosystems

Energy enters most ecosystems through primary production and moves through trophic levels while dissipating as heat.

Understand the mechanism

Producers capture energy; consumers obtain it through feeding; decomposers recycle matter; energy transfer between levels is inefficient and is often approximated near 10%, not fixed.

Gross primary productivity is the total energy fixed by producers; net primary productivity subtracts producers’ respiratory costs. Energy moves through food webs but matter is recycled via carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and water cycles. Only a fraction of energy is available to the next trophic level.

Apply it on the AP exam: Read food webs and energy pyramids; distinguish cycling matter from one-way energy flow.
Check your understanding: How do GPP and NPP differ?

Answer: NPP equals GPP minus respiration by producers.

Lesson 8.3

Population Ecology

Populations change as births, deaths, immigration, and emigration change abundance.

Understand the mechanism

Exponential growth occurs under unconstrained conditions; logistic growth slows near carrying capacity because resources become limiting.

Change in population size combines births and immigration minus deaths and emigration. Exponential growth assumes a roughly constant per-capita growth rate; logistic models incorporate carrying capacity. Carrying capacity changes as resources and conditions change and is not a universal fixed number.

Apply it on the AP exam: Interpret J-shaped and S-shaped curves and calculate population growth rates.
Check your understanding: What shape characterizes logistic growth?

Answer: An S-shaped curve under a simple fixed-K model.

Lesson 8.4

Effect of Density on Populations

Population regulation depends on density-dependent and density-independent factors.

Understand the mechanism

Competition, pathogens, and some predation effects strengthen with density; severe weather and disasters can affect populations regardless of density.

Density-dependent processes often include competition and disease transmission, which may intensify with crowding. Density-independent disturbances such as hurricanes may affect both small and large populations. Real systems can exhibit interactions between factors.

Apply it on the AP exam: Identify which mechanism is density-dependent and predict changes in population growth.
Check your understanding: Is a hurricane density-dependent?

Answer: Typically categorized as density-independent.

Lesson 8.5

Community Ecology

Species interactions shape community structure and niches.

Understand the mechanism

Competition, predation, herbivory, mutualism, commensalism, and parasitism influence abundance; resource partitioning can reduce competition.

Competition can produce exclusion or resource partitioning. Predators and herbivores influence prey and plant communities; parasites benefit while harming hosts, mutualists both benefit, and commensal interactions have minimal measured effect on one partner. Keystone species can have effects disproportionate to abundance.

Apply it on the AP exam: Classify interaction signs (+/−/0) and predict outcomes of species introduction or removal.
Check your understanding: What happens when competitors use different resources?

Answer: Resource partitioning can reduce direct competition.

Lesson 8.6

Biodiversity

Biodiversity includes genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity and supports resilience and ecosystem services.

Understand the mechanism

Species richness and evenness describe community composition; disturbance, habitat complexity, and conservation shape diversity.

Species richness counts species, whereas evenness considers how similarly individuals are distributed among them. Genetic diversity promotes variation within species; habitat diversity can support different ecological roles. Resilience and biodiversity are related but not interchangeable in every disturbance context.

Apply it on the AP exam: Use richness and relative-abundance data to compare communities; explain why richness alone can be misleading.
Check your understanding: Can two communities have equal richness but unequal diversity?

Answer: Yes; different evenness changes diversity.

Lesson 8.7

Disruptions in Ecosystems

Natural disturbances and human actions change ecosystem interactions and stability.

Understand the mechanism

Climate shifts, habitat fragmentation, nutrient pollution, invasive species, and succession can alter populations and nutrient cycles.

Primary succession begins where soil is absent; secondary succession follows disturbance where soil remains. Nutrient runoff may cause algal blooms and oxygen depletion. Fragmentation changes edge conditions and dispersal. Predict consequences through causal pathways and remember ecosystem recovery can be nonlinear.

Apply it on the AP exam: Use experimental or observational data to argue how a disruption affects ecosystem structure and recovery.
Check your understanding: Why can fertilizer runoff kill fish?

Answer: Eutrophication can promote decomposition and dissolved oxygen depletion.
