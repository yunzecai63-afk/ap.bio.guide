Populations evolve, not individuals

Keep mutation, recombination, selection, drift and gene flow conceptually separate. Read phylogenies by ancestry and apply Hardy–Weinberg assumptions carefully.

Practice scientific skills: Allele-frequency simulations; chi-square/genotype tests; phylogenetic evidence comparisons.

Scientific reasoning matters as much as vocabulary: interpret a model, identify variables and controls, calculate when appropriate, and use evidence to support a biological explanation.

Lesson 7.1

Introduction to Natural Selection

Evolution is a change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations, not a goal-directed change in individual organisms.

Understand the mechanism

Heritable variation plus differences in survival and reproductive success can alter allele frequencies.

Individuals vary in heritable characteristics, environments create differential survival and reproduction, and alleles associated with higher reproductive success can rise in frequency. Natural selection acts on expressed phenotypes, while populations—not individuals—evolve over generations.

Concept→Mechanism→Evidence→Prediction
Apply it on the AP exam: Identify variation, selection pressure, and fitness in a population scenario.
Check your understanding: Does selection cause useful mutations to appear?

Answer: No; selection acts on heritable variation that arises without regard to need.

Lesson 7.2

Natural Selection

Individuals with advantageous heritable traits tend to leave more surviving offspring in a given environment.

Understand the mechanism

Selection acts on expressed phenotypes, while evolutionary change appears as shifts in allele frequencies; environmental context determines which traits are favored.

Directional selection favors one end of a distribution; stabilizing selection favors intermediate forms; disruptive selection may favor extremes. Fitness means relative reproductive contribution in a particular environment, not strength or intentional improvement.

Apply it on the AP exam: Distinguish adaptation from intentional change or an organism gaining traits because it needs them.
Check your understanding: What does stabilizing selection favor?

Answer: Intermediate phenotypes.

Lesson 7.3

Artificial Selection

Humans choose which organisms reproduce, changing allele frequencies over generations.

Understand the mechanism

Selective breeding can accentuate desired traits while reducing genetic diversity and increasing linkage with undesirable traits.

Artificial selection occurs when humans choose which organisms reproduce, changing traits across generations. Selection can shift allele frequencies quickly, but restricted breeding may reduce diversity or enrich undesirable linked traits.

Apply it on the AP exam: Compare intentional human-mediated selection with natural environmental selection.
Check your understanding: What determines an artificial-selection trait?

Answer: Human choices about reproduction.

Lesson 7.4

Population Genetics

Population genetics examines allele frequencies and evolutionary forces.

Understand the mechanism

Mutation, gene flow, genetic drift, and natural selection alter allele frequencies; bottlenecks and founder effects intensify drift in small populations.

An allele frequency counts copies of an allele among all alleles at that locus; a genotype frequency counts individuals of a genotype. Migration can introduce alleles, drift can randomly change frequencies, mutation introduces variation, and selection systematically associates variation with reproductive outcomes.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict the effects of migration, chance events, or selection on genetic diversity.
Check your understanding: What does gene flow do?

Answer: Moves alleles between populations.

Lesson 7.5

Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium

The Hardy–Weinberg model provides a null expectation when evolutionary forces are absent.

Understand the mechanism

Under random mating, a very large population, and no selection, mutation, or migration: p+q=1 and p²+2pq+q²=1 for two alleles.

In an ideal Hardy–Weinberg population, p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1 for two alleles at a diploid autosomal locus. Required assumptions include no selection, mutation or migration, random mating and very large population size. Failure of equilibrium can indicate violation of assumptions but not which one by itself.

Apply it on the AP exam: Calculate carrier frequency from recessive-phenotype frequency and explain what violating assumptions implies.
Check your understanding: If q = 0.2, what is 2pq?

Answer: 0.32, because p = 0.8.

Lesson 7.6

Evidence of Evolution

Independent lines of evidence support evolution and descent with modification.

Understand the mechanism

Fossils, geographic distribution, anatomy, embryological patterns, and DNA/protein sequence similarity reveal patterns of ancestry.

Independent evidence lines include fossils, shared molecular sequences, homologous structures, vestigial structures and biogeography. Homology indicates shared ancestry, while analogy can arise independently under similar selective conditions. Evaluate how strongly each comparison supports a particular evolutionary claim.

Apply it on the AP exam: Judge how strongly different forms of evidence support an evolutionary relationship.
Check your understanding: How do analogous wings differ from homologous structures?

Answer: Similarity can come from convergent function without the same ancestral structure.

Lesson 7.7

Common Ancestry

Shared heritable features across species reflect descent from ancestral populations.

Understand the mechanism

Homologous structures and conserved molecular sequences indicate ancestry; analogous features can arise independently by convergent evolution.

Phylogenies represent hypotheses about common ancestry. A clade includes an ancestor and all descendants; sister taxa share an immediate common ancestor. Evidence from DNA and conserved proteins can test relatedness and reveal lineages not obvious from appearance.

Apply it on the AP exam: Distinguish homology from analogy and avoid equating visible similarity with closest ancestry.
Check your understanding: Do two sister taxa need to resemble one another visibly?

Answer: No; phylogenies infer ancestry using multiple evidence types.

Lesson 7.8

Continuing Evolution

Evolution continues as environments, variation, and selection pressures change.

Understand the mechanism

Antibiotic resistance and pesticide resistance spread when resistant heritable variants survive treatment and reproduce.

Pathogens can evolve drug resistance when preexisting or newly arising resistant variants reproduce preferentially under treatment. Environmental change can alter which trait is favorable. Evolutionary change can also arise from drift or gene flow; not all change is adaptation.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain why antimicrobial exposure selects existing variants rather than intentionally generating useful mutations.
Check your understanding: Why can resistance increase during treatment?

Answer: Resistant variants leave more offspring under that selective pressure.

Lesson 7.9

Phylogeny

Phylogenetic trees represent hypotheses of evolutionary relationships from shared ancestry.

Understand the mechanism

Nodes indicate common ancestors; sister groups share a more recent ancestor; branch rotation around a node does not change relationships.

Nodes on a tree represent common ancestors, while branches represent lineages. Rotating branches around a node does not change relationships. A more recent shared node indicates closer relatedness; terminal species at the right side are not inherently more advanced.

Apply it on the AP exam: Identify most recent common ancestors and avoid reading left-to-right as a ladder of progress.
Check your understanding: Can branch tips be read as evolutionary ranks?

Answer: No; tree layout is not a ladder.

Lesson 7.10

Speciation

Speciation occurs when populations become reproductively isolated and diverge.

Understand the mechanism

Geographic separation or barriers to mating can limit gene flow; prezygotic and postzygotic barriers maintain isolation.

Allopatric speciation begins with geographic isolation; sympatric speciation can occur within the same geographic region. Prezygotic barriers block mating or fertilization, while postzygotic barriers affect hybrid survival or fertility. Speciation is usually a process rather than a single instant.

Apply it on the AP exam: Explain how restricted gene flow and divergence can generate distinct species.
Check your understanding: Name one prezygotic isolation barrier.

Answer: Different mating times, for example.

Lesson 7.11

Variations in Populations

Variation arises through mutation and recombination and changes through evolutionary mechanisms.

Understand the mechanism

Genetic diversity influences responses to environmental pressures; random drift can eliminate alleles even if those alleles are not harmful.

Genetic variation comes from mutation and sexual recombination. Bottlenecks and founder events can sharply alter allele frequencies by chance, especially in small populations. Reduced variation can constrain future responses, but the effect depends on the environment and the trait.

Apply it on the AP exam: Predict how diversity affects a population's potential response to change without treating adaptation as guaranteed.
Check your understanding: Why are founder populations prone to drift?

Answer: A small random founding sample may not represent source allele frequencies.

Lesson 7.12

Origins of Life on Earth

Scientific hypotheses investigate how nonliving chemistry could give rise to self-replicating systems.

Understand the mechanism

Early Earth chemistry, simple organic molecules, protocell-like compartments, and RNA-related hypotheses are studied as possible stages; uncertainty remains.

Abiogenesis research studies plausible transitions from prebiotic chemistry to self-replicating and compartmentalized systems. Evidence motivates hypotheses concerning organic molecule production, mineral surfaces, membranes and RNA-like catalytic systems; there is no single experimentally established full pathway.

Apply it on the AP exam: Differentiate hypotheses about life's origin from evolutionary explanations of diversification after life emerged.
Check your understanding: Is one origin-of-life pathway proven?

Answer: No; multiple testable hypotheses remain.
